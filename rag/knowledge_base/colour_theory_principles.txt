COLOUR THEORY AND PSYCHOLOGY PRINCIPLES
Source: Itten, Albers, Brand Colour Research — Multi-Aesthetic Reference
Category: Design Theory — Colour

================================================================================
FOUNDATIONAL PRINCIPLES (ITTEN + ALBERS)
================================================================================

ITTEN'S SEVEN CONTRASTS — applied across brand aesthetics:

HUE CONTRAST: Pure colours at maximum saturation against each other.
Signal: Bold, energetic, populist. Used in mass-market, streetwear, fast food, youth culture.
Avoid in: Premium, minimal, luxury aesthetics.

LIGHT-DARK CONTRAST: Pairing light tones with dark for hierarchy.
The most universal contrast tool. Controls where the eye moves first.
Wide light-dark on narrow hue = quiet luxury. Wide light-dark on saturated hue = high fashion drama.

COLD-WARM CONTRAST: Cool-toned vs warm-toned colours.
Warm tones: approachable, safe, organic, sensorial. Cool tones: precise, modern, technical, clinical.
Mixing deliberately: warm background + cool accent = friendly competence (common in tech brands).

SATURATION CONTRAST: Saturated vs muted/desaturated.
Desaturated palette: restraint, intelligence, longevity. High saturation: urgency, energy, trend.
The rule: saturation level correlates inversely with perceived exclusivity in most categories.

SIMULTANEOUS CONTRAST (Albers): Adjacent colours alter each other's perceived hue.
The same taupe reads cooler on warm cream than on white. Test palettes in layout context, never in isolation.

EXTENSION CONTRAST: Proportion of each colour. 70% base / 20% structure / 10% accent is the quiet
luxury formula. Equal proportions across multiple colours = loudness.

================================================================================
COLOUR PALETTES BY AESTHETIC TERRITORY
================================================================================

--- QUIET LUXURY / PREMIUM WELLNESS ---
Palette character: Narrow hue range, muted, warm neutrals
Key tones: Warm cream (#F5F1E8), stone/sand (#D8C7AE), taupe (#9B8C7D), sage-grey (#6F7563), deep charcoal (#2B2B2B)
Psychology: Restraint as value signal. Quality communicated through absence of noise.
Avoid: Saturated accents, bright white, botanical greens at full saturation
Benchmark aesthetics: AURU, The Row, Aesop, Cos, Kinfolk, Bamford

--- HIGH FASHION / EDITORIAL ---
Palette character: High contrast, often monochromatic or two-tone extremes
Key tones: Pure black + white + one saturated or metallic accent; or deep jewel tones (burgundy, cobalt, forest) with black
Psychology: Drama, authority, aspiration. Colour is a weapon, not a background.
Key technique: Didone type on black reads as fashion; same type on cream reads as heritage luxury
Benchmark aesthetics: Celine, Bottega Veneta dark range, Vogue covers, Alexander McQueen

--- DARK LUXURY / MODERN VOID ---
Palette character: Near-black dominant, one warm accent maximum
Key tones: Deep charcoal, obsidian, dark slate, one warm neutral (parchment or aged brass)
Psychology: Exclusivity through inaccessibility. The visual equivalent of a members-only door.
Key technique: Warm light sources photographed against dark grounds — creates intimacy rather than coldness
Benchmark aesthetics: Le Labo, Byredo, Diptyque, Rick Owens, Tom Ford

--- BRUTALIST / RAW TECH ---
Palette character: Industrial, unfinished, intentionally harsh
Key tones: Concrete grey, black, raw white, acid yellow or safety orange as disruption accent
Psychology: Anti-aesthetic as aesthetic. Signals authenticity, no-bullshit positioning, technical confidence.
Typography colour: Black on white, white on black — no gradients, no soft tones
Benchmark aesthetics: The Brutalist web design movement, early Balenciaga campaigns, Bloomberg Terminal aesthetic, some Web3 branding

--- TECH / DIGITAL PRODUCT ---
Palette character: Clean, systematic, functional. Colour used for information hierarchy, not mood.
Key tones: White or very light grey base, mid-grey for secondary elements, one brand blue/purple/teal, semantic colours (red/green/amber) for status
Psychology: Trust through clarity. Every colour has a defined function. Decorative colour reads as unprofessional in SaaS.
Key technique: Accessible contrast ratios (WCAG AA minimum 4.5:1) as design constraint, not afterthought
Benchmark aesthetics: Linear, Notion, Figma, Stripe, Vercel, Arc browser

--- ARTISAN / HERITAGE CRAFT ---
Palette character: Aged, warm, earthy. Colours that look like they came from natural pigments.
Key tones: Ochre, terracotta, umber, aged cream, forest green, brick red, raw linen
Psychology: Authenticity, slowness, pre-industrial quality. Anti-generic. Signals something was made by hand.
Avoid: Bright white (too new), cool greys (too digital), black (too graphic/designed)
Benchmark aesthetics: Specialty coffee roasters (Blue Bottle early, Onyx, Madcap), craft beer packaging, artisan bakeries, organic farm brands

--- STREETWEAR / YOUTH CULTURE ---
Palette character: Bold, irreverent, often unexpected combinations
Key tones: Depends on season and brand — but often: black + white + one disruptive colour (acid green, hot pink, warning orange, electric blue)
Psychology: Identity and belonging. Colour signals which tribe you're in.
Key technique: Contrast for legibility at small sizes (for merch and social thumbnails) — function drives choice
Benchmark aesthetics: Supreme (red/white), Palace, Stüssy, Off-White, Aimé Leon Dore (more restrained end)

--- NORDIC / SCANDINAVIAN FUNCTIONAL ---
Palette character: Clean, light, functional. Inspired by northern light and natural materials.
Key tones: Cool white, warm white, pale birch, dusty sage, slate blue, soft grey
Psychology: Democratic design. Accessible intelligence. Form follows function, beauty follows form.
Avoid: Warm rich tones (reads as Mediterranean), dark heavy tones (reads as Gothic)
Benchmark aesthetics: Muji, Swedish Hasbeens, Snow Peak, Arc'teryx (product language), Marimekko (prints aside)

--- TROPICAL / MAXIMALIST RESORT ---
Palette character: Rich, saturated, layered. Multiple warm-vibrant tones together intentionally.
Key tones: Terracotta, warm coral, cobalt, palm green, sand, raw white
Psychology: Abundance and pleasure. More is more. Restraint reads as cold in this context.
Key technique: Warm base holds saturated colours together — a cool base would make them clash
Benchmark aesthetics: Cult Gaia, Lisa Says Gah, resort hotel identity, Havaianas premium range

================================================================================
ALBERS ON COLOUR PERCEPTION — PRACTICAL RULES
================================================================================

1. Colour is never seen in isolation — always in relation to its neighbours.
2. Approve palettes in full layout context, never from swatches on white.
3. The same muted tone reads sophisticated on warm cream, average on pure white.
4. Near-black (#2B2B2B, #1A1A1A) reads warmer and more editorial than #000000.
5. Two warm neutrals together intensify each other's warmth — useful for sensorial brand design.
6. Cool grey on warm background reads as a deliberate, modern interruption (good for tech-wellness hybrids).

================================================================================
COLOUR AND PERCEIVED BRAND POSITIONING — QUICK REFERENCE
================================================================================

PREMIUM SIGNALS: Narrow hue range. Low saturation. Clear light-dark hierarchy. Near-black not black. No trend colours.
MASS-MARKET SIGNALS: Wide hue range. Higher saturation. Bright white. Complementary contrasts. Colour-coding by product.
CLINICAL SIGNALS: Cool-toned palette. High black-white contrast. No warmth. Pharmaceutical white.
AUTHENTIC/CRAFT SIGNALS: Earthy, aged tones. Imperfect palette (not perfectly matched). Natural pigment references.
TECH SIGNALS: Systematic colour use. Functional colour hierarchy. Accessibility-compliant contrast. No decoration.
STREETWEAR SIGNALS: Bold contrast. Disruptive accent. Culturally specific colour references. Print-legible at scale.
EDITORIAL SIGNALS: Restrained palette with one intentional drama point. Colour serves composition, not the reverse.
